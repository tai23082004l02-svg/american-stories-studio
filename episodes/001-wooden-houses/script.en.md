## 01 — The house hiding in plain sight

The brick house across the street might be a wooden house.

Look past the brick, and you may find a timber frame doing the work of holding up the building. The brick is an outer layer, not a solid wall carrying the floors and roof.

That changes the question.

It isn't just why Americans build houses that look wooden. It's why wood is so often the skeleton underneath.

According to the National Association of Home Builders, analyzing Census Bureau data, ninety-four percent of single-family homes completed in twenty twenty-four used wood framing.

That figure is about newly completed single-family homes, not every home standing in America.

So how did a collection of boards become the default? The answer starts with a nineteenth-century change in how people put a house together.

Source notes: S1, S2. Brick-veneer example is conditional; percentage explicitly scoped to 2024 completed single-family homes.

## 02 — A building method that could spread

Picture two ways to make a wooden frame.

One uses large timbers, carefully shaped joints, and substantial carpentry work. The other uses smaller pieces of lumber, cut to standard dimensions and fastened with nails.

During the nineteenth century, machine-cut lumber and machine-made nails helped the second approach spread.

Balloon framing was an important part of that transition. Its exact origins are debated, so the story isn't as simple as one inventor changing everything on one day.

The important shift was practical: building with repeatable pieces.

Modern platform framing developed from that broader tradition. Instead of long wall studs running through multiple stories, each floor forms a platform for the next level.

The familiar construction-site skeleton is the result of an evolving building method, not an unfinished log cabin.

Source notes: S3, S4. Do not assign a single inventor; balloon framing and modern platform framing are distinct.

## 03 — The advantage is a system

Wood's usefulness isn't just about the material.

Standard lumber is relatively easy to transport and handle. Builders know how to work with it, and ordinary framing jobs don't require the same specialized equipment as every alternative construction system.

Those advantages reinforce each other.

If the people, tools, and available materials already fit a method, choosing it can simplify the next project. That is an explanation of how a building system stays familiar, not proof that wood always wins on price.

A particular site's costs still depend on the design, labor, material prices, and local conditions.

Think of the frame as part of a production process. A builder is choosing how a house will be assembled, not just comparing two objects labeled wood and concrete.

Source notes: S4. Familiarity reinforcing adoption is an editorial interpretation, not a measured causal share or cost comparison.

## 04 — The wall is more than its frame

Now look at a wall in cross-section.

The framing provides a structure. Other parts of the assembly have other jobs: insulation slows heat flow, protective layers manage exposure to weather, and the interior and exterior finishes complete the enclosure.

In a brick-veneer wall, the brick layer has its own support and connects back to the framed wall through anchors.

So brick on the outside doesn't tell you everything about the structure behind it.

And a wood frame doesn't automatically tell you whether a house will be comfortable.

Wood studs themselves conduct more heat than the insulation between them. They can create thermal bridges: paths that let heat move through the wall.

Continuous exterior insulation is one way to reduce that effect.

The useful comparison is the complete wall assembly, not a close-up of one board or one brick.

Source notes: S2, S5. Frame, cladding and insulation are separate functions; no claim that studs are insulation.

## 05 — Sometimes better means using less

Here's a counterintuitive detail: adding more lumber isn't always an improvement.

Advanced framing techniques aim to place structural members where they are needed while leaving more room for insulation.

Depending on the design and local requirements, that can mean different stud spacing, more carefully planned corners, or less unnecessary framing around openings.

It can also reduce the amount of material crews have to cut and install.

This isn't permission to remove a stud from an existing wall. The framing layout has to be designed and checked for the building.

But it shows why the material alone is an incomplete measure of quality.

A carefully designed arrangement of smaller members can do a useful job. More pieces, heavier pieces, and better construction are three different ideas.

Source notes: S6. Advanced framing is design-dependent; not a DIY instruction or universal stud-spacing prescription.

## 06 — Light doesn't mean connections don't matter

Of course, a lightweight frame still has to handle forces.

During strong winds, the question isn't simply whether an individual board is strong enough. Forces have to move through the building: from the roof, into the walls, and down to the foundation.

That chain is called a continuous load path.

Connections between those parts are critical. A weak connection can interrupt the chain, even when the pieces on either side look substantial.

Guidance from the Building America Solution Center emphasizes roof-to-wall, wall-to-wall, and wall-to-foundation connections.

That doesn't make a wood-framed house storm-proof. Site hazards, design, construction, and maintenance still matter.

It means a finished house should be judged as an interconnected structure, not as a pile of materials ranked by how heavy they feel.

Source notes: S7. Load path and connections reduce vulnerability; no hurricane/tornado survival guarantee.

## 07 — The trade-offs are real

None of this makes wood indestructible.

Moisture and wood-degrading organisms are real concerns. The Forest Service explains that fungi need suitable conditions, including moisture, to grow.

Keeping the structure appropriately protected is part of making wood last. A neglected water problem doesn't become harmless because the frame is hidden.

Fire is another real concern. Wood is combustible.

But wildfire exposure also involves the roof, vents, windows, exterior materials, nearby vegetation, and combustible items around the building.

CAL FIRE's home-hardening guidance addresses those entry points and exposures, including embers getting into vulnerable spaces.

A label like wood house or brick house can't replace that more detailed picture.

The trade-off isn't imaginary. It just isn't explained well by a single dramatic clip of a building failing.

Source notes: S8, S9. Explain risks without comparing unverified fire ratings or prescribing retrofit dimensions.

## 08 — Why it remains the default

So why do so many American homes have wood frames?

Part of the answer is historical: smaller, standardized pieces changed how houses could be assembled.

Part is practical: a familiar method built around manageable components.

And part is architectural: the frame works with other layers that handle weather, insulation, and appearance.

That doesn't mean the same choice is right for every site. The national statistics also show regional differences, and alternative framing methods remain part of the market.

Nor does a cheaper or more convenient frame guarantee an affordable finished home. Framing is only one decision in the whole project.

The more useful question is what sits behind the finish, how the parts connect, and whether the assembly fits the place it was built.

Next time you see a brick facade, remember: you may be looking at the outside of the story.

The structure that explains the house could be hiding just behind it.
